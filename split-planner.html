How the numbers are calculated

The planner mirrors the way the split tool itself divides a file. It takes the middle of your min/max range as the target, divides the total duration by that target and rounds up to get a whole number of parts, then spreads the video evenly across them. If the even parts would come out shorter than your minimum, it falls back to the maximum length and recalculates. The last part is simply whatever remains, which is why it can be shorter than the others.

The start and end times in the table are the requested cut points. Because Reel Cutter copies existing compressed frames rather than re-encoding, each real cut snaps back to the nearest keyframe at or before the requested time. That is why the planner asks for your keyframe interval: with a 2-second interval, any individual part can begin up to roughly 2 seconds earlier than the table shows. Phone and screen-recorder footage commonly uses 1–2 second intervals; some editing exports and older cameras use much longer ones, in which case your parts will drift further from the plan. The keyframe guide explains this in detail.

Reading the size estimate

If you enter the source file size, the planner divides it by the video's duration to get an average bitrate and applies that to each part. Real files vary — a talking-head segment compresses far better than a fast-moving scene, so individual parts can be noticeably larger or smaller than the average. Treat the figure as a planning guide for storage and upload time, not as an exact number.

The planner also warns when a source file is large, because the split tool holds the file in your browser's memory while it works. There is no fixed limit: it depends on your device, browser and what else is open. As a practical rule of thumb, phones are the most likely to struggle with multi-gigabyte files, while a desktop browser with spare memory usually copes with much more. If a split fails, the troubleshooting guide lists what to try.

Choosing a length range

The presets in the goal menu come from the ranges discussed in our clip-length guide. They are starting points, not platform rules; platforms change their upload limits and recommendation behaviour from time to time, so check each platform's current help pages before committing to a long posting plan. A useful habit is to run the planner twice with two different ranges and compare the part counts, so you can see how much posting material each choice creates from the same recording.

Planner FAQ

Does the planner upload my video?
No. It does not even read a video file — it only does arithmetic on the numbers you type in, in your browser.
Why is my last part shorter than the rest?
The last part holds whatever is left after the even divisions. If the leftover is very small, try widening your min/max range so the parts divide more evenly.
Where do I find my keyframe interval?
Most phone and screen-recording footage uses 1–2 seconds, so the default of 2 is a safe estimate. If you are unsure, leave it at 2 and expect cuts to land within a couple of seconds of the plan.
Can I use this for videos I don't own?
Reel Cutter is meant for footage you created or have the right to reuse — your own recordings, lectures, podcasts and home movies. Splitting someone else's copyrighted film or show and posting it can infringe their rights.
