Video format glossary

Plain-English definitions for the terms that actually come up when splitting, exporting, or troubleshooting video files.

Video terminology gets thrown around a lot in editing apps and error messages without much explanation. This reference covers the terms that matter most for understanding how splitting, compression, and playback actually work — each one links back to where it's relevant in our other guides.

Container vs. codec

These two get mixed up constantly, but they're different things:

  • Container (also called "wrapper" or "format") — the file structure that organizes video, audio, subtitles, and metadata together. MP4, MOV, MKV, and AVI are all containers. A container is like a shipping box: it holds the content but doesn't determine how that content is compressed.
  • Codec (short for "coder-decoder") — the actual compression method used on the video or audio data inside the container. H.264, H.265 (HEVC), VP9, and AV1 are video codecs; AAC and MP3 are audio codecs. Two MP4 files can use completely different codecs inside the same container type.

This distinction matters for splitting: Reel Cutter works with the MP4/MOV container format directly, and supports the video/audio codecs most commonly found inside them (H.264, H.265, AAC, and similar).

Keyframe (I-frame)

A complete, self-contained frame that doesn't depend on any other frame to be decoded. Keyframes are the only points a video can be cut cleanly without re-encoding, since every frame between keyframes only stores "what changed" relative to frames before it. See our full keyframes explainer for how this affects split precision.

GOP (Group of Pictures)

The span of frames between one keyframe and the next. A "GOP length" of 60 at 30fps means a keyframe every 2 seconds. Shorter GOPs mean more frequent keyframes (larger files, but more precise cutting and seeking); longer GOPs compress more efficiently but make lossless splitting less precise.

Bitrate

How much data is used per second of video, usually measured in Mbps (megabits per second). Higher bitrate generally means better visual quality at the cost of larger file size, though the relationship depends heavily on the codec and content — a static talking-head video needs far less bitrate than fast motion or complex textures to look equally sharp.

Re-encoding vs. byte-copy (stream-copy)

  • Re-encoding — fully decoding a video back to raw frames and recompressing it, often at a different bitrate, resolution, or codec. Necessary for real edits (cropping, filters, changing resolution) but introduces generational quality loss and takes significant processing time.
  • Byte-copy (stream-copy) — copying the already-compressed video and audio data directly into a new file without decoding it. Much faster and lossless, but limited to operations that don't require touching individual frames' content — like splitting at keyframe boundaries, which is exactly what Reel Cutter does.

Timescale

The unit MP4 files use internally to measure time, expressed as "ticks per second" rather than a fixed unit like milliseconds. A common video timescale is 30,000 ticks per second for 29.97fps footage. This is largely invisible to viewers, but it's part of why some frame-accurate timing calculations in video tools look like odd fractions internally.

Fragmented MP4 (fMP4)

A variant of the MP4 container that spreads its sample index across many small chunks (moof/mdat pairs) throughout the file, instead of one master index at the front. Common in browser-recorded video, some screen-capture tools, and streaming pipelines. Reel Cutter supports both fragmented and standard MP4 structures.

Frame types: I, P, and B frames

  • I-frame (keyframe) — a complete image, decodable on its own.
  • P-frame — stores only the difference from the frame before it.
  • B-frame — stores differences relative to both a previous and a following frame, generally the most compressed but most dependent frame type.

Most video is a repeating pattern of these three, forming the GOP structure described above.

Resolution vs. quality

Resolution (like 1080p or 4K) describes pixel dimensions, not visual quality directly. A heavily compressed 4K file can look worse than a well-encoded 1080p file at a fraction of the size. When splitting, resolution has no effect on precision — only the codec's keyframe placement does.
