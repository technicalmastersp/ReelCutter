Best clip length for Reels, TikTok & Shorts

Platforms keep raising their technical maximums, but the length that actually gets watched hasn't moved nearly as much. Here's what to aim for when you set up a split.

Every major short-form platform now technically allows much longer uploads than when Reels, TikTok, and Shorts launched. That doesn't mean you should use the full ceiling — discovery algorithms still lean hard toward shorter, tightly-paced clips. Here's the practical picture as of 2026, and how to translate it into settings for the split tool.

Why length affects reach, not just runtime

Every platform's recommendation algorithm leans heavily on completion rate — the percentage of viewers who watch a clip all the way through — and rewatch rate, since a viewer who loops a clip signals strong engagement. A 3-minute clip that loses half its audience by the 20-second mark sends a weaker signal than a 20-second clip that most viewers finish. That's the core reason shorter clips tend to get pushed to more non-followers even when a platform's technical length cap is much higher: the algorithm isn't rewarding "short" for its own sake, it's rewarding a high finish rate, and shorter clips make that easier to achieve.

Instagram Reels

Instagram now allows Reels up to around 20 minutes when uploading a pre-recorded file, up from a 90-second cap just a couple of years ago. In practice, though, Instagram's discovery algorithm favors much shorter runtimes for reach to non-followers — commonly cited sweet spots are roughly 7–30 seconds for broad discovery and up to about 60–90 seconds for tutorial-style content. Runtimes beyond a few minutes tend to reach mostly your existing followers rather than new viewers, since Explore-page distribution weighs completion rate heavily.

TikTok

TikTok supports much longer uploads than it started with — up to several minutes when recording in-app, and considerably longer for uploaded files. Even so, TikTok's own historical engagement data has repeatedly pointed to short clips (well under a minute) performing best for completion rate and replays, which are strong ranking signals on the platform. Longer TikToks can still perform well, but they generally need a stronger hook in the first 1–2 seconds to hold viewers into a longer runtime.

YouTube Shorts

YouTube Shorts allows videos up to about 3 minutes, a significant jump from its original 60-second limit. Average watched Shorts still tend to run closer to 20–40 seconds, and anything under roughly 5 seconds is unlikely to gain traction. YouTube's Shorts shelf also considers whether a Short drives viewers to a creator's longer-form content, so a Short that teases a longer video can justify a slightly longer runtime than one meant to stand alone.

A practical rule of thumb

Goal Suggested length
Maximize reach / new viewers7–30 seconds
Tutorials / how-to steps30–90 seconds
Deep dives for existing followers90 seconds–3 minutes
Cross-posting the same cut everywhere15–60 seconds (safest across all three)

Note that platform limits and algorithm preferences change fairly often — treat the numbers above as a snapshot rather than gospel, and check each platform's current help pages before planning a long-running content strategy around them.

A simple way to test what works for your audience

Platform-wide averages are a starting point, not a guarantee for your specific channel. A lightweight way to find your own sweet spot:

  1. Split one long recording into two batches with different target lengths — say, 20–30 seconds and 60–90 seconds — using two separate runs of the split tool.
  2. Post both batches over a similar time period and note completion rate / average watch time from each platform's built-in analytics (Instagram Insights, TikTok Analytics, YouTube Studio all report this per video).
  3. After a few weeks, lean your default split length toward whichever range consistently retains more of your specific audience — your niche, pacing, and content type all shift where the real sweet spot lands.

Common questions

Is there a real penalty for uploading a video that's too long?
Not a direct one — platforms won't reject or de-prioritize a video purely for its length within the allowed maximum. The "penalty" is indirect: longer videos are statistically more likely to have lower completion rates, and completion rate is what actually affects distribution.
Should every part from a split series be the same length?
Not necessarily. It's fine for the last part of a series to be shorter — most audiences don't mind a shorter finale, and forcing an even length can mean awkward cuts mid-sentence or mid-scene.
Does clip length affect ad revenue on any of these platforms?
On platforms with in-stream or mid-roll ad monetization for longer videos, runtime can matter for ad eligibility — check each platform's current monetization requirements, since these change independently of the length limits covered here.
